# CzeV343: when and how does the quadruple fall apart? {#czev343}

## The system and the question {#czev343-system}

CzeV343 (V849 Aur) is a doubly eclipsing quadruple. Two eclipsing binaries orbit each other
(Pejcha et al. 2022), so the hierarchy is 2+2:

- **Binary A**: period 1.2093 d, eccentricity 0.114, apsidal period 33 yr.
- **Binary B**: period 0.8069 d, circular. The ratio of the two periods is close to 3:2.
- **Mutual orbit of A and B**: period 1454 d (4.0 yr), eccentricity 0.70, inclined only about
  12° to the plane of the sky. Both binaries are seen nearly edge-on, so their orbital planes are
  strongly misaligned with the mutual orbit: 76–104° for A, and 63–86° or 94–117° for B.

Pejcha et al. fitted isochrones to the light of the four stars and gave two solutions (their
Table 2):

- **Fiducial**: age about 630 Myr; A: 1.76 + 1.27 M☉; B: 1.36 + 1.20 M☉.
- **Alternative**: age about 140 Myr; A: 2.48 + 1.42 M☉; B: 1.75 + 1.38 M☉.

They followed both binaries with the binary population synthesis code COMPAS (Riley et al. 2022),
whose stellar and binary prescriptions descend from SSE/BSE (Hurley et al. 2000, 2002), on a grid
around the measured parameters. In ≳ 85 % of their realisations binary A widens significantly by
mass transfer, to a largest separation of 50–220 R☉; about 60 % of the realisations of binary B
widen to 80–150 R☉. Most of these binaries later merge; about 20 % of A survive as double white
dwarfs. Pejcha et al. applied the triple stability criterion of Mardling & Aarseth (2001) to each
binary, with the other binary as a point mass. They found that the quadruple stays dynamically
stable only while $a_A, a_B \lesssim 50$ R☉, and concluded that "essentially all realizations of
binary A that survive main sequence as a binary will experience this dynamical instability". They
did not follow the system further: "This instability has not been explored so far in 2+2
quadruples".

Our question has two parts: **when** does the instability start, and **what happens then** –
ejection of a star, a collision, an exchange of partners, or a new stable configuration?

## Method: two stages {#czev343-method}

Integrating four stars directly over 2 Gyr with orbital periods of one day is not feasible. The
problem separates into two timescales, so we split it into two stages.

**Stage 1 – stellar evolution and mass transfer (millions of years).** We take 300 draws for each
of the two isochrone solutions. In each draw:

- the mass of each binary varies by 10 % (Gaussian σ; Pejcha et al. estimate an uncertainty of about
  10 % for the stellar masses from the choice of metallicity);
- the mass ratio within each binary varies by ±5 %;
- the age is multiplied by a log-normal factor (σ = 0.3 in ln age), because isochrone ages are
  uncertain;
- the orientations (nodes) of the two binaries are drawn at random. The nodes fix the mutual
  inclinations, which the stability criteria depend on.

For each draw SWINGERsim computes the mass-transfer history of both binaries up to 4 Gyr from now:
stellar tracks (MIST), Roche-lobe overflow, the stability and rate of the transfer, contact and
common envelope (*Physics and Methods*, chapter Mass transfer and evolution). Along each history it
evaluates the two stability criteria of both nested triples – each binary with the other binary as a
point mass:

- **Mardling & Aarseth (2001) criterion (MA01)**: a ratio of the outer periastron to the critical
  value. Below 1 the triple is unstable.
- **Vynatheya et al. (2022) criterion (V22)**: a fit to many N-body integrations, with the same
  meaning of "below 1".

The outer orbit follows the masses adiabatically ($a_\mathrm{out} M_\mathrm{tot}$ = const for
isotropic mass loss). The first time either ratio drops below 1 is the **onset of instability**.

A draw that does not finish within 300 s of computing time, usually because the stellar track
leaves the table, is recorded as unfinished. This affected 12 fiducial and 14 alternative draws
(4–5 %). We see no reason why these draws should differ systematically from the others.

**Stage 2 – dynamics after the onset (thousands of years).** For six onsets we set up a direct
N-body integration at that moment. These are five fiducial onsets spread over the onset times and
the one alternative onset that reached N-body. The binary that widened (always A) stays a binary,
with the masses, separation, radii and temperatures of its history. The other binary, B, is
16–20 times tighter than the outer periastron ($a_B/a_\mathrm{out} \approx 0.005$), so we treat it
as a single body. Its "radius" is its separation, so that a close approach to B counts as a
collision. General relativity, tides and stellar rotation are switched on. Each onset is integrated
for $10^4$ yr (about 2500 outer orbits) as an ensemble of 100 members. The members differ in the
orbital phases of both orbits and in the argument of periastron of A, which are not determined by
the evolution.

One quantity in stage 2 needs separate treatment. The donor of binary A is, at the onset, a
**stripped giant**: 0.26–0.30 M☉, 13.4–14.5 R☉, 3850–3980 K. It has lost almost all of its
envelope to its companion, which now has 2.6–3.0 M☉. How strongly such a star is distorted by its
companion depends on its internal mass concentration, measured by the **apsidal-motion constant**
$k_2$. A star with all its mass at the centre has $k_2 = 0$, a uniform sphere has 0.75, and
main-sequence stars have about 0.005–0.02. The tidal and rotational bulges make the orbit of A
precess (apsidal motion), and this precession competes with the gravitational pull of B (see
below). The value of $k_2$ for this star is not in any table, so we ran every onset with three
values: 0.001, 0.01 and 0.05. That makes 18 ensembles with 1800 members in total.

**What is SWINGERsim's own work here.** These parts were programmed for SWINGERsim:

- the mass-transfer histories: stability of the transfer, rates, contact, common envelope, and the
  coupling to the stellar tracks;
- the evaluation of both stability criteria along the histories;
- the construction of the N-body state at the onset;
- the ensemble machinery and its statistics;
- the apsidal-constant model of the next section.

The stellar tracks come from MIST (Choi et al. 2016). The N-body integration uses the IAS15
integrator of REBOUND (Rein & Spiegel 2015), with relativity (`gr_full`) and the self-consistent tides and
spins (`tides_spin`; Lu et al. 2023) of REBOUNDx (Tamayo et al. 2020).

**Binary A almost always interacts.** Its heavier star (1.76 M☉ in the fiducial solution) fills its
Roche lobe while it still burns hydrogen in its core. This is **case A** mass transfer, typical of
periods of about a day.

- In the **fiducial** solution the transfer most often brings the two stars into contact. The binary
  then merges after a common envelope (59 %, median 1.4 Gyr from now, 16–84 %: 1.0–2.0 Gyr) or
  remains a contact binary to the end of the computation (30 %).
- In the **alternative** solution the stars are more massive and younger, and binary A becomes a
  contact binary in 99 % of draws.

In both cases the separation stays small: in panel (b) the tall peaks near $\log a \approx 0.85$
(7 R☉) are these draws.

**Only a stripped donor widens the orbit.** In 10 % of fiducial draws the donor loses its whole
envelope before contact; these draws have the heaviest donors (≳ 1.8 M☉). The transfer then
reverses the mass ratio and the orbit widens to 53–66 R☉: the second peak in panel (b), right of
the dashed line. These draws, and a few that reach contact only after widening to above 50 R☉, are
the 29 % beyond 50 R☉.

Half of the draws beyond 50 R☉ (14 % of all) cross the stability limit. The other half do not,
because the critical separation depends on the mutual inclination and on the outer eccentricity at
that moment. The onsets fall between 1.0 and 2.1 Gyr from now (panel c); the fiducial median is
1.49 Gyr. At the onset binary A has a separation of 57–62 R☉. It is always binary A that triggers
the instability: binary B becomes a contact binary (fiducial 70 %, alternative 95 %) without ever
widening.

**The two criteria disagree at the onset.** By construction MA01 equals 1.00 at the onset, but
V22 is still 1.19–1.24, so by Vynatheya et al. the system is still stable. The criteria were
calibrated on different sets of integrations and differ by about 20 % near the boundary. This is
why stage 2 is needed: the N-body integration decides, not the criterion.

**Which criterion to trust here.** A systematic test answers this for CzeV343 after the mass
transfer: binary A of 2.77 + 0.26 M☉ with periods of 12–80 d, binary B of 1.36 + 1.20 M☉ with 4 d,
outer eccentricity 0.5–0.8, isotropic orientations, chosen so that the MA01 ratio lies between 0.6 and
1.6. All 1200 members were integrated for 200 outer orbits (800 yr) with MEGNO. The 243 members that
stayed intact but were chaotic or below the MA01 limit were integrated for $10^4$ outer orbits
($4 \times 10^4$ yr). In addition, the ghost-orbit test of Vynatheya et al. (2023), on which their
neural network was trained, was run for every member. Stars are point masses with small radii
(0.05 R☉), so ZLK collisions are recorded but are not counted as dynamical break-ups.

Against the break-ups within $4 \times 10^4$ yr (245 members):

- MA01 (ratio < 1) finds 98 % of them and flags 32 % of the members that do not break up.
  Its best threshold is 0.89, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.93.
- V22 (ratio < 1) finds 51 % and flags 6 %. Its best threshold is about 1.17.
- The neural network of Vynatheya et al. (2023) finds 25 %. Even for the label it was trained on
  (the ghost test, 198 unstable members) it finds only 32 %.
- These members lie just outside its training set: the mass ratio within binary A is 10.7, the
  training set has at most 10.

For a 2+2 with one widened, strongly unequal binary, MA01 is therefore the safe criterion: it hardly
misses a break-up and errs on the side of instability. V22 at its nominal threshold misses half of
them. The onset of CzeV343, MA01 = 1.00 and V22 = 1.19–1.24, lies where 5–11 % of such systems
break up within $4 \times 10^4$ yr. This agrees with stage 2, where none of the 1800 members broke up
in $10^4$ yr.

**Comparison with Pejcha et al. (2022).** The two studies agree on three points:

- the instability, if it comes, is caused by binary A widening beyond about 50 R☉;
- most realisations of binary A end in a merger;
- binary B does not trigger the instability first.

They differ in how often A widens before it merges: ≳ 85 % in COMPAS, but only 10–29 % here. Here
most histories reach contact while both stars are still on the main sequence. B does not widen at
all here, while 60 % of the COMPAS realisations of B widen to 80–150 R☉. Both codes describe mass
transfer with parametrised prescriptions, not with detailed stellar models. They differ in the
stellar tracks (MIST here, SSE fitting formulae in COMPAS) and in how the start of contact and its
consequences are treated.

**The stellar tracks decide.** To separate the two, the same 300 draws of the fiducial solution were
run again with SSE tracks (the Hurley et al. 2000 fitting formulae on which COMPAS is built) instead of
MIST, with everything else unchanged (**Table 2a**). With SSE, binary A leaves a stripped donor in
82 % of the draws, and its largest separation exceeds 50 R☉ in 83 % (median 65.6 R☉, against 7.3 R☉
with MIST). This is the widening that COMPAS finds in ≳ 85 % of its realisations. The outcome of the
two track families agrees in only 77 of the 288 draws that finished with both (27 %). 137 draws that
end in a common-envelope merger with MIST leave a stripped donor with SSE. With SSE the mass transfer
starts later (median 70 Myr), and 75 % of the quadruples become dynamically unstable (1.25–2.00 Gyr,
16–84 %) against 14 % with MIST. The difference with COMPAS is therefore a difference of the stellar
tracks, through the response of the donor and so the stability of its mass transfer, not of the
dynamics. Which track family describes these stars better is a question for detailed binary-evolution
models.

An independent check is the grid of detailed case-A binary models by Nelson & Eggleton (2001). For
initial mass ratios near 1.4 (here 1.76/1.27) and the shortest initial periods, their models
typically "come into contact slowly, but reach contact before either star has left the main
sequence" (their subtype AS). Their paper gives no separations, so it cannot say how far the orbit
widens on the way. But contact on the main sequence is the outcome that dominates our histories,
and it is the opposite of the widening that dominates in COMPAS. Our largest separations, 53–66 R☉, lie at the lower end
of the COMPAS range. Whichever code is closer to the truth, the conclusion of this test is the
same: when A widens, the quadruple crosses the MA01 limit at a separation of about 57–62 R☉.

## The internal structure of the stripped donor {#czev343-k2}

Before looking at stage 2 we estimate the apsidal constant of the donor, because the result
depends on it.

At the onset the stellar track gives a helium core of 0.258 M☉ inside a star of 0.262 M☉. Only
about 0.004 M☉, or 1.5 % of the mass, remains in the convective envelope, which reaches out to
14 R☉. We computed $k_2$ for such a star with a simple two-zone model (`tools/k2_condensed.py`):

- a compact core of radius 0.03 R☉;
- an envelope that is a polytrope of index $n$ in the gravity of the core ($n = 1.5$ for a
  convective envelope, $n = 3$ for comparison).

The program integrates the Clairaut–Radau equation for the tidal distortion outwards through the
star (Kopal 1959). The result, $k_2 = (3-\eta_R)/(2(2+\eta_R))$, follows from the logarithmic
derivative $\eta_R$ of the distortion at the surface. A uniform sphere gives the textbook 0.75,
which checks the integration.

envelopes of different mass. Columns:

- $M_\mathrm{env}$: mass of the envelope;
- $M_\mathrm{env}/M$: its fraction of the stellar mass;
- $k_2$ ($n$ = 1.5): convective envelope;
- $k_2$ ($n$ = 3): radiative-like envelope.

With the envelope the track predicts, $k_2 \approx 6 \times 10^{-4}$, even below the lowest value
we integrated. To reach $k_2 = 0.01$ the star would need an envelope of about a quarter of its mass,
which a stripped donor does not have. Observed stripped donors on their way to becoming helium
white dwarfs, such as the pre-helium white dwarfs in EL CVn binaries, keep hydrogen envelopes of
only about 0.001–0.005 M☉ (Maxted et al. 2014). We therefore regard
**$k_2 \approx 0.001$ as the realistic case**, and 0.01 and 0.05 as deliberately generous upper
bounds.

The model has two limits. It ignores the gravity of the envelope itself (≤ 1.5 % of the mass, a
negligible error here) and the rotational flattening of the core. Both change $k_2$ by much less
than the factor of ten between our three values.

Table 3 and Figure 2 show a sharp dichotomy.

**With the realistic $k_2 = 0.001$ every member ends in a collision** of the two stars of
binary A. The collisions happen 53–550 years after the onset, with most of them within the first
150 years (panel b). The cause is visible in panel (a): the eccentricity of A grows from zero to
about 0.7 within a few decades. This is the **von Zeipel–Lidov–Kozai (ZLK) mechanism**. A distant
third body on an orbit strongly inclined to the inner binary exchanges angular momentum with it and
periodically raises the inner eccentricity at the expense of the inclination. At the onset the
mutual inclination of A and the outer orbit is 76–97° (medians of the six onsets), close to the
90° at which ZLK is strongest. Today the ZLK timescale of A is about 650 yr (Pejcha et al. 2022), much longer
than its 33-yr apsidal period, so ZLK is quenched now; at the onset, with A eight times wider and
its period 24 times longer (30 d instead of 1.2 d), the ZLK timescale, which scales as $P_\mathrm{out}^2/P_A$, has shrunk to about 650/24 ≈ 27 yr. The steps in the curve are the outer periastron passages: the outer
orbit is so eccentric (0.70) that most of the perturbation acts during them.

At $e_A = 0.70$ the periastron distance of A is $a(1-e) \approx 0.3 \times 57.6 \approx 17$ R☉.
That is just the sum of the two radii (14.0 + 3.7 R☉), so the giant touches its companion and the
integration records a collision. In reality this means a merger or a common envelope of the donor
with its companion.

**With $k_2 = 0.01$ or 0.05 the system survives** $10^4$ yr intact (598 of 600 members with
0.05). The eccentricity of A stays below 0.25 (0.01) or 0.05 (0.05). The reason is apsidal
precession. ZLK works only if the orientation of the inner orbit stays coherent long enough for the
third body to pump its eccentricity. Any additional apsidal precession faster than the ZLK cycle
washes the effect out (Holman et al. 1997; Fabrycky & Tremaine 2007). The column *apsidal period*
in Table 3 shows the competing rates: the ZLK eccentricity grows within about 50–60 yr in panel (a).

- With $k_2 = 0.01$ the tidal and rotational bulges of the giant turn the orbit in 59 yr, about as
  fast as ZLK, so the eccentricity grows only to about 0.1–0.25 and oscillates (blue curve).
- With 0.05 the precession takes 12 yr and ZLK is fully quenched.
- With 0.001 it takes 589 yr, far too slow to matter.

General relativity adds a precession with a period of about $2.5 \times 10^5$ yr, irrelevant here.

The two late collisions with $k_2 = 0.05$ (after 7200 and 8900 yr, in one onset) come from a slow
chaotic drift of the outer orbit and show that even the stiffest giant is not safe forever. They
are 0.3 % of the members.

**The quadruple is not torn apart.** In none of the 1800 members is a star ejected, nor do the
binaries exchange partners. The instability flagged by MA01 at the onset does not mean an immediate
dynamical break-up. With V22 still above 1, the four-body configuration remains bound and
hierarchical over $10^4$ yr. What happens is slower and secular: the third body drives the
eccentricity of the widened binary until its stars collide.

**Numerical quality.** The relative energy error stays below $7 \times 10^{-8}$ for $k_2$ = 0.001
and 0.01, and below $1.4 \times 10^{-6}$ for 0.05, where the stiffer bulges require more
integration steps. Both are far below anything that could affect the outcome.

## What the stage 2 model leaves out {#czev343-limits}

- **Mass transfer during stage 2.** At the onset the donor still slightly overfills its Roche lobe
  (by 20–40 % in radius at zero eccentricity). The N-body integration keeps the masses fixed. Over
  the 50–550 yr until the collision the transfer moves only of order $10^{-6}$ M☉, so it cannot
  change the outcome. But it means the giant is in contact with its Roche lobe throughout, and the
  collision is in fact the onset of a common envelope.
- **Binary B as one body.** B is 16–20 times tighter than the outer periastron. Its internal
  structure affects the outer orbit only through its quadrupole moment, which is smaller than that
  of A by $(a_B/a_A)^2 \approx 10^{-2}$.
- **Tidal dissipation.** Tides are conservative here (no time lag). Dissipation would act on
  timescales of $10^4$ yr or more for a 14 R☉ giant at 57 R☉ and could only circularise A between
  ZLK cycles. That matters for $k_2 \geq 0.01$, where the system survives, not for the realistic
  case.
- **Only six onsets.** They span the range of onset times and both solutions, and give the same
  result for each $k_2$ (panel b of Figure 2 combines them). We see no sign that more onsets would
  change the picture.

## Is CzeV343 in the 3:2 resonance? {#czev343-resonance}

The periods of the two binaries, 1.2093 and 0.8069 d, are close to 3:2. A first-order resonance
would show as a libration of one of the angles

$$\varphi_1 = 3\lambda_A - 2\lambda_B, \quad \varphi_2 = \varphi_1 - \varpi_A, \quad
\varphi_3 = \varphi_1 - \varpi_B ,$$

where $\lambda$ is the mean longitude and $\varpi$ the longitude of the periastron. In the present
system all three angles circulate, with periods of 1.6–2.0 yr (400 yr of full physics). The present
system is not in resonance.

How far is it from the resonance? 141 integrations of 1000 yr scanned the period of A from 1.20995
to 1.21030 d in steps of $2.5 \times 10^{-6}$ d (energy error below $10^{-10}$). The mean rate of
each angle falls linearly with $P_A$, by 4702 rad/yr per day. For $\varphi_1$ and $\varphi_3$ it
passes through zero without a plateau, at 1.2101625 and 1.2100224 d: these resonances, if present, are
narrower than the step. For $\varphi_2$ the rate stays at zero over four steps, from 1.210125 to
1.2101325 d, instead of changing by ±0.017 rad/yr. There the angle librates with an amplitude of
66–330° and does not complete a turn in 1000 yr. This is the 3:2 Lindblad resonance that Tremaine
(2020) identifies in 2+2 quadruples, whose argument contains the periastron of the binary with the
longer period, here A, at first order in its eccentricity. Its width is about $10^{-5}$ d in $P_A$,
or $8 \times 10^{-6}$ in the period ratio. CzeV343 lies $7.9 \times 10^{-4}$ d, about 80 widths,
below it.

The outer orbit splits the resonance into satellites at $3n_A - 2n_B - \dot\varpi_A + k\,n_\mathrm{out}
= 0$, spaced by $0.47\,|k|\,\epsilon^{3/2}$ in $1 - 2P_A/3P_B$ (Tremaine 2020; $\epsilon \approx
0.007$ here), with a strength proportional to $e_\mathrm{out}^{|k|}$. With $e_\mathrm{out} = 0.70$ they
are not weak. In the 400-yr run of the present system all the angles $\varphi_2 + k\lambda_\mathrm{out}$
with $k = -6 \ldots +6$ circulate. The slowest, $k = -2$, has a period of 11.4 yr. Its period ratio
near 3:2 is therefore not the result of a present resonance. Why so many 2+2 quadruples lie near such
ratios (Kolář et al. 2025) remains open.

## Answer {#czev343-answer}

- In most futures of CzeV343 the quadruple does **not** fall apart dynamically at all. Binary A
  merges (fiducial solution, median 1.4 Gyr from now) or becomes a contact binary (alternative
  solution), and the system continues as a hierarchical system with one merged or contact pair.
- **Dynamical instability** occurs in 14 % of the fiducial and 1 % of the alternative draws,
  1.3–1.6 Gyr (fiducial, 16–84 %) from now. In these draws the donor of A is stripped and the
  binary widens to 57–62 R☉.
- **What follows** depends on the internal structure of the stripped giant. With the envelope its
  track predicts ($k_2 \approx 10^{-3}$), ZLK cycles driven by binary B raise the eccentricity of A
  to 0.7. The giant collides with its companion within 50–550 years: a merger, or a common envelope
  inside a quadruple. Only if the giant kept a quarter of its mass in the envelope ($k_2 \gtrsim
  0.01$) would its tidal bulge quench ZLK and keep the system together for at least $10^4$ yr.
- No ejection or exchange of stars was found in any of the 1800 integrations.
- How often binary A widens depends on the stellar tracks: in 10 % of the draws with MIST, in 82 %
  with SSE on the same draws, which explains the difference from COMPAS.
- CzeV343 is not in the 3:2 resonance; it lies about 80 resonance widths away.

The prediction is testable in principle on other systems: a stripped red giant in a 2+2 quadruple
with a strongly inclined outer orbit should be rare, because it would not survive long.

**Gaia note.** The Gaia DR3 astrometric solution (Gaia Collaboration 2023) of CzeV343 has a renormalised unit weight error
(RUWE) of 1.62. Values above about 1.4 indicate that a single-star model does not fit the motion of
the light centre (Lindegren et al. 2018). For CzeV343 this is consistent with the 4-yr mutual orbit
of the two binaries at the Gaia parallax of 0.224 ± 0.025 mas. We did not use it quantitatively.
