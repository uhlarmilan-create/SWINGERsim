# CzeV4315: a 2+2 quadruple or a 2+(2+1) quintuple? {#czev4315}

## The system and the question {#czev4315-system}

CzeV4315 (HD 228777) contains two eclipsing binaries (Zasche et al. 2025):

- **Binary A**: period 6.73907 d, eccentricity 0.64, inclination 87.44 ± 0.21°. It has eclipsed
  steadily for as long as it has been observed.
- **Binary B**: period 0.9193238 d, circular. Its inclination grew from below 57.4° to
  63.42 ± 0.25° within a few years, so its eclipses deepen at a rate of about 1.4° per year.
  The times of its eclipses also show a **119-day periodicity**.

The inclination of B was below 57.4° in TESS sector 15 (2019) and grew through 58.6° (2021), 60.3°
(2022) and 62.2° (2024) to 62.9° (TESS sector 81, 2024) (Zasche et al. 2025, their Table 3). The
paper gives no amplitude of the 119-day signal and no spectroscopy.

Zasche et al. left open how the two binaries are arranged; they called the two possibilities
"2+2+1" and "2+1+2". They noted one weak hint in favour of the second: A shows no fast apsidal
motion, which a strong dynamical interaction with B would cause. Two arrangements explain the
119-day signal:

- **2+2**: A and B orbit each other with a period of 119 d. The 119-day signal is the light-travel
  time and the dynamical perturbation of this mutual orbit.
- **2+(2+1)** (the "2+1+2" of Zasche et al.): B has its own third star (m5) on a 119-day orbit
  (called Bt below). This triple B+m5 then orbits binary A on a much wider, unknown orbit.

A fast change of inclination needs a strong perturber close to B. The question is which
arrangement can turn B by 1.4°/yr **without** turning A as well, and without destroying the
system.

**Masses and radii.** These are estimates made here, not values from the paper. Each binary was
assumed to have a primary of about 10 000 K (spectral type B9 V); the masses follow from the
measured mass ratios and the radii from the relative radii $R/a$. The values are 2.40 + 2.09 M☉
for A and 2.30 + 2.35 M☉ for B, with radii of 1.6–1.8 R☉.

## Method: the same data, two hierarchies {#czev4315-method}

We built one ensemble for each hierarchy and weighted every member by the same observations:

1. **Inclination change of B.** The rate of change of $|i_B - 90°|$ today must be −1.4 ± 0.2°/yr,
   which enters as a Gaussian likelihood. The negative sign means that B turns towards edge-on.
2. **A keeps eclipsing.** Integrated backwards, A must have eclipsed for at least the last
   5 years. Integrated forwards, it must keep eclipsing for at least 3 months. Members violating
   either condition get zero weight.
3. **No past mass transfer.** No star may have filled its Roche lobe during the backward
   integration, because both binaries are detached.
4. **Survival.** The system must be intact in both directions of time. A member that collides or
   loses a star within the integrated span cannot be the system we see.

Both ensembles keep only members that are stable by the criterion of Vynatheya et al. (2022) at the
start. The 2+2 ensemble includes tides and rotation because its 119-day mutual orbit is tight. In
the quintuple the tidal effects on the orbital planes are negligible over 60 years compared with
the perturbation by m5. The shorter span of the quintuple is still longer than any of the
constraints, which reach at most 5 years back.

**Both hierarchies can turn B fast enough.** In Figure 3a both histograms reach the observed band,
so the rate of B alone does not decide.

**In a 2+2 the same perturbation also turns A.** The 141 members of the 2+2 ensemble that match
the rate of B fall into two groups:

- 66 of them end in a collision, of the two stars of B or of a star of B with A. At a period of
  119 d the mutual orbit lies close to the stability limit, and an eccentric mutual orbit drives the
  stars together. This is why 57 % of all 2+2 members collide (panel b).
- The other 75 survive, but in all of them A tilts by 2–50° per year, typically 10–20°. A would
  stop eclipsing within months (median 0.4 yr), whereas it has eclipsed for years.

The reason is simple. In a 2+2 with a 119-day mutual orbit, A is the third body of B and B is the
third body of A, at the same distance. A perturbation strong enough to turn B by 1.4°/yr turns A
too: A's orbit is wider (6.7 d against 0.9 d) and therefore even more sensitive. No 2+2 member
satisfies all the observations. The four non-zero weights are below $3 \times 10^{-13}$.

**In a 2+(2+1) the perturber acts on B alone.** The third star m5 sits on its 119-day orbit around
B only. This is also the arrangement for which Zasche et al. saw a "weak indirect hint": A shows no
fast apsidal motion. They left the question open. A is far away, at 4000–50 000 d (16–84 %) in the weighted members, and its inclination
changes by less than 0.22°/yr in every matching member. 81 % of all members and 99 % of the
weight remain intact (panel b).

**Evidence.** The mean weights are $Z = 0.022$ for 2+(2+1), carried by 46 effective members, and
$3 \times 10^{-16}$ for 2+2. The 2+2 value rests on effectively one member and is therefore only
an upper estimate. A safer statement is this: with 1000 members and no member giving a weight
above $10^{-12}$, the evidence of the 2+2 is below about $10^{-3}$, otherwise at least one member
would have matched. The Bayes factor in favour of 2+(2+1) is therefore at least about 20 by this
conservative bound, and formally $10^{13}$–$10^{14}$. Either way the data clearly prefer a
third star around B.

The comparison has two caveats. The quintuple ensemble has no tides and a shorter span than the
2+2 ensemble (Table 4). Requiring survival changes its evidence by less than 1 % (0.0216 instead of
0.0218), and tides cannot rescue the 2+2, whose problem is the tilt of A, not survival.

**The mass of the third star alone is not determined.** In Figure 4b the weighted distribution
rises above the prior only for $m_5 \gtrsim 1$ M☉ and then fills the prior up to its upper edge at
6 M☉. 16–84 % lies at 1.4–4.9 M☉, and 95 % at 0.75–5.7 M☉. Values below about 0.7 M☉ are
excluded: a light star cannot turn B fast enough.

Panel (a) shows why the upper side stays open. The rate at which B's plane precedes grows with the
mass of the perturber and with $(1 - e_\mathrm{Bt}^2)^{-3/2}$, and it depends on the mutual
inclination through $\cos i_\mathrm{mut}$. A light star on an eccentric orbit (upper left) and a
heavy one on a nearly circular orbit (lower right) produce the same rate. The data constrain this
combination, not the mass. A first ensemble with the mass limited to 3 M☉ seemed to place $m_5$
near 2–2.8 M☉. That was the edge of the prior, not a measurement, which is why the prior was
widened here.

**This makes a testable prediction.** 74 % of the weight lies at $m_5 > 2$ M☉. Such a star is
about as massive as the stars of B (2.3 M☉) and, if on the main sequence, about as bright. It
would show up as:

- third light in the light curve of B;
- a third set of spectral lines;
- a light-travel-time effect in the eclipse times of B. For the median posterior (m5 = 3.2 M☉) the
  semi-major axis of the 119-day orbit is about 0.94 au and B moves on 0.38 au around the centre of
  mass, which gives up to about 3 minutes, times sin of the inclination of Bt. The 119-day timing
  signal of Zasche et al. can therefore be checked against this once its amplitude is measured.
  Radial velocities of B would vary over 119 d with a semi-amplitude of up to about 35 km/s.

A spectrum that shows only the four stars of A and B would push $m_5$ to the lower end, about 1 M☉, and therefore to a high eccentricity of Bt
(Figure 4a, upper left).

**Prediction for the eclipses.**

- In the weighted members B stops or changes its eclipsing regime within about 30–60 years
  (16–50 %); in the remaining third nothing changes within the 60 years integrated.
- A keeps eclipsing in all weighted members over the whole span.

## The fifth star seen by Gaia {#czev4315-gaia}

Zasche et al. (2025) noted that CzeV4315 is a visual double with a separation of about 1.9″ and
almost no relative motion in the Washington Double Star Catalog, which suggests an orbital period
of thousands of years. Gaia DR3 (Gaia Collaboration 2023) resolves this companion 1.84″ from CzeV4315. It has G = 11.92 (CzeV4315 itself has G = 9.65) and a
parallax of 0.826 ± 0.036 mas, equal within the errors to that of CzeV4315 (0.893 ± 0.014 mas,
about 1.1 kpc). It is therefore a physical companion at a projected distance of about 2000 au.
Being 2.3 mag fainter, it cannot be one of the binaries, whose stars are similar. It is also too
far away to be the 119-day star m5. It is another member of the system, which makes CzeV4315 at
least a sextuple in the 2+(2+1) picture. It does not affect the dynamics on the timescales studied
here. Being 2.3 mag fainter, it adds about 11 % to the light of every photometric measurement that does not
resolve it (TESS pixels are 21″), which is part of the large third light in the TESS fits.
